What we teach

Six topics, each taught with real numbers and real forms. Pick the one you need right now, or come to all of them.

FAFSA help

High school seniors and college students

We sit down with you and your parents and fill it out together, from creating your account to the last signature. If you're already in college, you need to file it again every year, and we help with that too.

The 2027–28 FAFSA opened on September 24, 2026. Filing early matters, because some state and school aid runs out.

Net price vs sticker price

High school juniors and seniors, families

A college's sticker price is what it charges. The net price is what you pay after grants and scholarships, and it's the number to compare between schools. Aid letters list loans and work-study next to grants, so we show you how to pull them apart.

$58,400 sticker price − $31,000 in grants = $27,400 net price per year. A $5,500 loan on the same letter isn't a discount. You pay it back with interest.

Budgeting and saving

Anyone with a job, a refund check or an allowance

We start from what actually lands in your account, not your hourly wage. Then we decide where each dollar goes before it's spent: bills, everyday spending, and savings for the things that always come up.

On the sample pay stub above, $480 of work becomes $412.88 after taxes. That's the number your budget is built on.

Credit vs debit

High school seniors and college students

A debit card spends money already in your account. A credit card borrows money you have to pay back, and if you don't pay the full balance each month, you're charged interest on what's left. We cover how credit scores work, why paying on time matters most, and how overdraft fees can hit debit cards too.

Paying the full statement balance every month means you pay no interest on your purchases, while still building a credit history.

Taxes

Students with a first job, and their families

What a W-4 is, why money comes out of every paycheck, and how filing a return works. We also show families which tax information the FAFSA asks for, so the two forms make sense together.

The $18.40 in federal tax withheld on the sample stub isn't final. When you file a return, you may get some or all of it back.

Investing 101

College students and older high schoolers

What stocks, index funds and retirement accounts are, how they're different from a savings account, and why starting early matters more than starting with a lot.

$50 a month from age 18 to 65 could grow to about $219,000. Start at 28 instead and it's about $105,000, even though you'd only have put in $6,000 less.Assumes a steady 7% yearly return. Real markets go up and down, and returns aren't guaranteed.

Why we started

Our founder had two acceptance letters and two aid offers, and parents who had never seen either kind before. She picked the school with the bigger aid number. More than a third of that aid turned out to be loans.

When she started helping other students with their letters, they kept asking about everything else too: their first paycheck, a credit card offer in the mail, whether they had to file taxes. Finance Forward teaches all of it, because nobody else in the house was going to.

It started with two letters on a kitchen table

It was April of her senior year. She had two acceptances and two financial aid letters, and her parents, who had worked for years to get her to that point, had never been to college themselves. They sat at the kitchen table and tried to read the letters together. Nobody in the room knew what a subsidized loan was.

One school offered $39,000 in total aid. The other offered $31,000. That felt like an easy choice, so she picked the bigger number.

She didn't really understand what she'd signed until sophomore year, when the first loan statement showed up. More than a third of that aid had been debt. The school she turned down would have cost her family less every year she was there.

Nobody had misled her. Everything was printed on the page. What she didn't have was someone to tell her that "aid" doesn't mean free, that work-study is a job and not a discount, and that the most useful number, the net price, usually isn't printed on the letter at all.

So she started small. First a spreadsheet for a younger cousin. Then a Saturday morning in a public library meeting room with eleven seniors, a stack of their real award letters and a box of highlighters. By lunchtime, three of those students had changed their minds about where to enroll.

That Saturday became Finance Forward. Before long, students were bringing more than aid letters: a first pay stub, a credit card offer that showed up in the mail, a question about whether they had to file taxes. So we started teaching those too. Today our sessions cover six topics, from the FAFSA to investing, and we still bring the highlighters.
